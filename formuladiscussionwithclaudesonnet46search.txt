## Understanding the Swiss Tournament Framework Formulas

---

### Foundational Scaling Factor: $n \times \frac{4}{3}$

Before diving into individual formulas, a key recurring element is $n \times \frac{4}{3}$. This inflates the raw player count $n$ by a factor of $\frac{4}{3}$ as a conservative buffer, acknowledging that Swiss pairings are never perfectly efficient (some matchups carry less discriminatory power), so the effective pool behaves statistically larger than $n$ alone.

---

### $k$ — Extra Rounds for Top $p$ Placements

$$k = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } \lceil p \rceil < 3 \\ \lceil \log_2(\lceil p \rceil \times \frac{4}{3}) \rceil - 2 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

- For $p < 3$ (determining only rank 1 or 2), no extra rounds are needed beyond the baseline — Swiss naturally resolves this.
- For $p \geq 3$, you need additional rounds because more players must be mutually separated. The $\lceil \log_2(p \times \frac{4}{3}) \rceil$ term mirrors the idea that resolving $p$ placements requires roughly as many rounds as running a mini-tournament among those $p$ players (scaled by $\frac{4}{3}$). Subtracting 2 removes the baseline already counted elsewhere.
- $k$ grows slowly (logarithmically) — doubling $p$ only adds one extra round.

---

### $c$ — Extra Rounds for Opponent Restriction

$$c = \lceil d \rceil - 1$$

A very direct formula: if you want the top $p$ players to avoid opponents from $d$ score groups away starting from the second-to-last round, you need $d - 1$ extra rounds of "buffer" time to enforce that separation. Each additional score group of separation costs one extra round.

---

### $R_{min}$ — Minimum Rounds Required

$$R_{min} = \min\!\Bigl(\underbrace{\text{odd}(n) \;?\; n : n-1}_{\text{(A) parity cap}},\quad \underbrace{\lceil \log_2(n \times \tfrac{4}{3}) \rceil + k}_{\text{(B) information-theoretic bound}}\Bigr)$$

**Term (A) — Parity Cap:**
This is the hard upper limit on meaningful Swiss rounds. In a Swiss tournament of $n$ players:
- If $n$ is **odd**, every round has every player paired, so the maximum useful rounds is $n$ itself.
- If $n$ is **even**, one player always gets a bye, so the cap is $n - 1$.

Beyond this cap, pairings repeat and rounds carry no new discriminatory value.

**Term (B) — Information-Theoretic Bound + Extra:**
- $\lceil \log_2(n \times \frac{4}{3}) \rceil$ is the core Swiss round count: the number of rounds needed so the number of possible distinct win records ($2^r$ levels) covers the effective player pool ($n \times \frac{4}{3}$). This generalises the classical $\lceil \log_2(n) \rceil$ formula with the $\frac{4}{3}$ buffer.
- Adding $k$ extends this for the need to resolve not just 1st place but the top $p$ placements. More placements to resolve = more rounds needed.

**Taking the MIN:** The formula ensures you never prescribe more rounds than physically make sense for the field size, but also never fewer than needed to distinguish the top $p$. $R_{min}$ is the **smallest round count that is both informationally sufficient and physically feasible**.

---

### $R_{max}$ — Maximum Recommended Rounds

$$R_{max} = \min\!\Bigl(\underbrace{n \text{ or } n-1}_{\text{(A)}},\quad \underbrace{2\lceil \log_2(n \times \tfrac{4}{3}) \rceil - 2}_{\text{(B)}},\quad \underbrace{R_{min} + c}_{\text{(C)}}\Bigr)$$

**Term (A):** Same parity cap as in $R_{min}$.

**Term (B) — Redundancy Threshold:**
$$2\lceil \log_2(n \times \tfrac{4}{3}) \rceil - 2 = \lceil \log_2(n \times \tfrac{4}{3}) \rceil + \underbrace{(\lceil \log_2(n \times \tfrac{4}{3}) \rceil - 2)}_{\text{this is the second inner expression}}$$
This is the point at which a second full logarithmic "cycle" of Swiss rounds has been completed (minus the 2-round baseline). Beyond this, the marginal discriminatory power of additional rounds becomes negligible — the standings have already been refined to the maximum extent Swiss pairing can achieve.

**Term (C) — Restriction Budget:**
$R_{min} + c$ caps $R_{max}$ at exactly the number of rounds needed to implement the $d$-score-group opponent avoidance restriction for the top $p$ players. Beyond $R_{min} + c$, the opponent restriction has been fully applied and further rounds add nothing to placement accuracy under those constraints.

**Taking the MIN:** $R_{max}$ is the **smallest of three "diminishing returns" ceilings** — parity, full information saturation, and restriction budget. More rounds beyond $R_{max}$ are genuinely redundant.

---

### Relationship Between $R_{min}$ and $R_{max}$

| Property | $R_{min}$ | $R_{max}$ |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Minimum to resolve top $p$ | Maximum before redundancy |
| Extra rounds | $+k$ (placement complexity) | $+k$ and up to $+c$ more (restriction buffer) |
| Gap | — | $R_{max} - R_{min} \leq c = \lceil d \rceil - 1$ |

The gap between them is always bounded by $c$, meaning the opponent-avoidance restriction $d$ is what gives you wiggle room between the minimum and maximum recommended round counts.

---

### $E_{R_{min}}$ and $E_{R_{max}}$ — Suggested Elimination Rounds

$$E_{R_{min}} = \left\lceil \frac{\lfloor R_{min}/2 \rfloor}{2} \right\rceil, \qquad E_{R_{max}} = \left\lceil \frac{\lfloor R_{max}/2 \rfloor}{2} \right\rceil$$

These suggest how many single-elimination rounds should follow the Swiss stage:
- First, $\lfloor R/2 \rfloor$ halves the round count (roughly the number of rounds that generated a competitive top half).
- Then, $\lceil \cdot / 2 \rceil$ halves again and rounds up, yielding approximately a **quarter** of the Swiss rounds as elimination rounds — a conservative and proportionate playoff stage.

---

### $L_{max}$ Family — Maximum Tolerable Losses

All $L_{max}$ variants share the same logic. For a given round count $r$ and player pool of size $N$:

**Step 1 — Find minimum wins $W$:**
$$W = \text{BINOM.INV}\!\left(r,\; 0.5,\; \frac{p}{N \times \frac{4}{3}}\right)$$
This finds the smallest $W$ such that $P(X \leq W) \geq \frac{p}{N \times 4/3}$ for $X \sim \text{Binomial}(r, 0.5)$.

By the symmetry of the Binomial at $p=0.5$, this implies the expected number of players achieving $\geq r - W$ wins is approximately $p$ — meaning $W$ losses is right at the boundary of the top $p$.

**Step 2 — Boundary correction:**
$$L_{max} = \begin{cases} W & \text{if } N \times \frac{4}{3} \times P(X \leq W) \leq p \\ W - 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
If the expected count at $W$ losses already overshoots $p$, pull back by 1 loss for safety.

$$T_i = \begin{cases} i & \text{if } i \leq 3 \\ \max(4,\; \lceil \log_2(i/3) \rceil + 3) & \text{if } i > 3 \end{cases}$$

### The Draw Problem: Shrinking Discriminatory Capacity

In a **no-draw** Swiss tournament, each round produces a binary outcome (win/loss). With $r$ rounds, there are $2^r$ distinct score levels, supporting up to $2^r$ players with full discrimination:

| Rounds $r$ | Max players (no draws) |
|---|---|
| 2 | 4 |
| 3 | 8 |
| 4 | 16 |
| 5 | 32 |
| 6 | 64 |
| 7 | 128 |

When **draws are possible**, outcomes are no longer purely binary — a draw is a half-point result that compresses the score spread, causing score groups to **collide and overlap** more. The discriminatory capacity per round shrinks, and the same $r$ rounds can now only reliably support $\frac{3}{4}$ of the original player count:

| Rounds $r$ | Max players (no draws) | Max players (with draws) |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 4 | 3 |
| 3 | 8 | 6 |
| 4 | 16 | 12 |
| 5 | 32 | 24 |
| 6 | 64 | 48 |
| 7 | 128 | 96 |

$$\text{Max players with draws} = 2^r \times \frac{3}{4}$$

---

### Why This Produces the $\frac{4}{3}$ Factor in the Formulas

The formulas need to solve the **inverse problem**: given $n$ players (in a draw-possible competition), how many rounds $r$ are required?

Starting from:
$$n = 2^r \times \frac{3}{4}$$

Solving for $r$:
$$2^r = n \times \frac{4}{3}$$
$$r = \log_2\!\left(n \times \frac{4}{3}\right)$$

This is exactly why $n \times \frac{4}{3}$ appears universally throughout the formulas — it is the **draw-adjusted effective player count** that must be fed into the base-2 logarithm. The $\frac{4}{3}$ is not a heuristic buffer, but the **exact multiplicative inverse of the $\frac{3}{4}$ capacity shrinkage** caused by draws.

---

### Conceptual Summary

| Perspective | Expression | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Capacity with draws | $2^r \times \frac{3}{4}$ | How many players $r$ rounds can discriminate |
| Rounds needed for $n$ players | $\log_2(n \times \frac{4}{3})$ | Inverse: how many rounds $n$ players need |
| The $\frac{4}{3}$ factor | $\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{-1}$ | Exact correction for draw-induced score compression |

So every instance of $\lceil \log_2(n \times \frac{4}{3}) \rceil$ in $R_{min}$, $R_{max}$, and the $L_{max}$ family is fundamentally saying: *"treat this draw-eligible field of $n$ players as equivalent to a no-draw field of $n \times \frac{4}{3}$ players when computing round requirements."*

### The Midpoint Property

The draw-adjusted capacity $2^r \times \frac{3}{4}$ is **exactly the arithmetic mean** of two consecutive no-draw capacities $2^r$ and $2^{r-1}$:

$$\frac{2^r + 2^{r-1}}{2} = \frac{2^r + \frac{2^r}{2}}{2} = \frac{2^r \cdot \frac{3}{2}}{2} = 2^r \cdot \frac{3}{4}$$

This holds perfectly across the entire table:

| $r$ | $2^{r-1}$ (lower) | $2^r$ (upper) | Midpoint $= 2^r \times \frac{3}{4}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 2 | 4 | **3** |
| 3 | 4 | 8 | **6** |
| 4 | 8 | 16 | **12** |
| 5 | 16 | 32 | **24** |
| 6 | 32 | 64 | **48** |
| 7 | 64 | 128 | **96** |

---

### Conceptual Meaning

This midpoint property has a compelling intuitive interpretation:

- **$2^r$** represents full binary discrimination — every round produces a decisive win or loss, maximally separating players
- **$2^{r-1}$** represents what capacity would be if an entire round were "wasted" — carrying no discriminatory information at all
- **The midpoint $2^r \times \frac{3}{4}$** says that draws effectively **cost exactly half a round's worth of discriminatory power**

In other words, the possibility of draws is equivalent to saying that, on average, **every two rounds, one half-round is lost to score compression**. The tournament functions as if it only has $r - \frac{1}{2}$ fully decisive rounds, which lands capacity exactly halfway between the $r$-round and $(r-1)$-round benchmarks.

---

### A Unified View

$$\underbrace{2^r \times \frac{3}{4}}_{\text{draw-adjusted capacity}} = \underbrace{\frac{2^r + 2^{r-1}}{2}}_{\text{midpoint between consecutive powers of 2}}= \underbrace{2^{r - \frac{1}{2}} \times \frac{\sqrt{2}}{1} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{...}}_{\approx 2^{r-0.415}}$$

More cleanly, since $\log_2\!\left(\frac{3}{4}\right) \approx -0.415$, draws effectively reduce the round count by about **0.415 rounds** in logarithmic terms — and the $\frac{4}{3}$ correction in the formulas is precisely what adds that fractional round back.

## Deriving $N_{min}$ and $N_{max}$

$$\lceil \log_2(n \times \tfrac{4}{3}) \rceil = r - k$$

### Solving the Inequality

The ceiling condition $\lceil x \rceil = r - k$ means:

$$r - k - 1 < \log_2\!\left(n \times \frac{4}{3}\right) \leq r - k$$

$$2^{r-k-1} < n \times \frac{4}{3} \leq 2^{r-k}$$

$$\frac{3}{4} \times 2^{r-k-1} < n \leq \frac{3}{4} \times 2^{r-k}$$

$$\boxed{N_{max} = \left\lfloor \frac{3}{4} \times 2^{r-k} \right\rfloor}$$
